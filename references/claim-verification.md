# Claim verification

Use this section when asked to verify a claim, alongside the quotation procedure in `SKILL.md`. The goal is to make it hard to hallucinate and easy for the reader to check. Quotations are observations; your interpretation belongs outside them. If you cannot find a passage supporting a point, say so. You may offer an explicitly labelled inference, but do not present it as a source-backed finding.

## Principles in the sources' words

### *The Scout Mindset*, Julia Galef

[Goodreads quotes index](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/65572070-the-scout-mindset-why-some-people-see-things-clearly-and-others-don-t), page date not stated.

> So I’ve given it one. I call it scout mindset: **the motivation to see things as they are, not as you wish they were.** Scout mindset is what allows you to recognize when you are wrong, to seek out your blind spots, to test your assumptions and change course.

Epistemic context: reader-curated quote index, not a paginated primary edition; the entry begins here, so no earlier sentence is supplied.

### *A new way to explain explanation*, David Deutsch

[Official TED transcript](https://www.ted.com/talks/david_deutsch_a_new_way_to_explain_explanation/transcript), talk delivered July 2009.

> And that's possible because the details of the original myth are unrelated to seasons, except via the myth itself. This easy variability is the sign of a bad explanation, because, without a functional reason to prefer one of countless variants, advocating one of them in preference to the others is irrational. **So, for the essence of what makes the difference to enable progress, seek good explanations, the ones that can't be easily varied, while still explaining the phenomena.** Now our current explanation of seasons is that the Earth's axis is tilted like that, so each hemisphere tilts towards the sun for half the year, and away for the other half.

Epistemic context: official transcript of a spoken talk, lightly edited for readability; “verbatim” here means to the transcript, not necessarily the live utterance.

### *A Technical Explanation of Technical Explanation*, Eliezer Yudkowsky

[LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/afmj8TKAqH6F2QMfZ/a-technical-explanation-of-technical-explanation), page date not stated here.

> I cannot properly convey just how ridiculous the notion would be, to a true Bayesian; there are not even words in Bayes-language to describe the mistake… **For every expectation of evidence there is an equal and opposite expectation of counterevidence.** If A is evidence in favor of B, then not-A must be evidence in favor of not-B. The strengths of the evidences may not be equal; rare but strong evidence in one direction may be balanced by common but weak evidence in the other direction. But it is not possible for both A and not-A to be evidence in favor of B. That is, it’s not possible under the laws of probability theory.

Epistemic context: author's own explanatory essay; the ellipsis is retained from the existing varglight excerpt, not independently checked against the linked source in this revision.

## For each source

Present the generous quotation and visible citation, then a short `epistemic context` note about the source: primary measurement, press release, self-report, secondary summary, or a quotation of someone else's view. Say who is endorsing the claim. The note describes the source and how it could know, rather than declaring that the claim is true.

Keep any editorial to one plain lead-in and one takeaway where possible. If a quotation has been shortened or clarified, use the marked edits and original-passage record from `SKILL.md`; never put an unmarked paraphrase inside a quotation.

## Epistemic summary

After the sources, give a short summary covering the relevant checks:

- **Who says it, and how they could know:** identify the primary observation and the path from it to the quoted source. Distinguish measurement, citation, hearsay, and self-report.
- **Independence:** do the sources report independent observations, or repeat one origin? Name the shared origin. Two indexes of the same announcement do not provide two independent observations.
- **Hard-to-vary check:** would the evidence distinguish the claim from its negation, or fit both about equally well?
- **Counterevidence:** what finding would change your mind? Absence matters only if you looked where evidence would be expected.
- **Assessment:** state what is supported, what remains uncertain, and the cheap way to be wrong. Use calibrated language; if a probability is useful, give a justified range rather than a precise-looking point estimate.

Do not assign numerical evidence weights to every quotation. Keep assessment in the final summary, anchored in the quotations rather than replacing them.
